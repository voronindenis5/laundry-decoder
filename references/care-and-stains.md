# Laundry Science & Practice — Reference

## 1. The care-label system

Care symbols are standardized internationally (ISO 3758, based on the
GINETEX system). Five families:

| Shape | Domain |
|---|---|
| Tub (with water) | Washing — dots/number = max °C, bars = mildness |
| Triangle | Bleaching |
| Square (± circle) | Drying — circle = tumble, lines = line/flat |
| Iron (with dots) | Ironing temperature |
| Plain circle (± letters) | Professional textile care (P, F, W, A) |

The symbols are **process ceilings**, not recommendations: a tub with "40"
means *don't exceed* 40°C, not "must be exactly 40". Washing colder is
nearly always safe (the exceptions: heavy soil/oil loads and sanitizing,
which genuinely need heat).

## 2. Temperature by number of dots

- **Wash tubs (Europe)**: number printed in tub = °C max.
- **American labels**: 1–6 dots mapped to 30/40/50/60/70/95°C.
- **Irons**: 1 dot = 110°C (synthetics), 2 dots = 150°C (wool/blends),
  3 dots = 200°C (cotton/linen).

## 3. Why the load-planner rules exist (the textile science)

- **Color bleeding**: loose dye molecules migrate in the wash water. New
  cotton (esp. red/black) has excess unreacted dye; hot water opens fibers
  and accelerates migration. Rule: bleeders wash alone (cold, with a
  color-set soak: 1 cup vinegar or salt bath per gallon, 30 min) the first
  two washes.
- **Whites vs darks**: even broken-in darks release micro-amounts of dye
  every wash; whites gradually gray. With hot water this is much faster.
  Whites-only loads also permit hot washes (60–95°C) that sanitize and
  keep whites bright via oxygen bleach.
- **Delicates + heavy**: towels and jeans are abrasive in the drum. A lace
  bra or silk blouse in that load comes out pilled and snagged.
- **Wool + terry**: wool felts with heat + agitation + moisture (scales
  interlock); terry maximizes agitation. Silk loses luster to alkaline
  detergents — needs pH-neutral (wool/silk) detergent.
- **Hot-only items**: kitchen towels, gym synthetics, sick-bed linen need
  ≥60°C for hygienic or de-oiling reasons; they can't share a 30°C load.

## 4. Detergent physics — why more is worse

Surfactants form micelles above the critical concentration; extra detergent
past that point adds nothing but redeposits onto fabric when it can't all
rinse (the "sour towel" smell). Follow the line on the cap for your water
hardness. Hard-water areas need more (ions eat surfactant) — a water
hardness test strip is a genuinely useful $5 purchase.

## 5. Stain chemistry — the four families

| Family | Examples | Chemistry of removal |
|---|---|---|
| Protein | blood, egg, milk, sweat | Enzymes or cold water; **heat sets** |
| Tannin/pigment | wine, coffee, tea, tomato, grass | Acid + oxygen bleach; hot sets some |
| Oil/grease | dressing, makeup, motor oil | Solvents + degreaser (dish soap); dry-then-treat |
| Combination | chocolate, gravy, latex paint | Split approach: solvent then enzyme |

Rules that save clothes:
1. **Work from the outside in** — dabbing outward spreads the stain.
2. **Back of the fabric** — flush the stain *out*, not through.
3. **Never dry until gone** — the dryer is a heat-set machine.
4. **Test seam/hidden area first** with any chemical.
5. **Least destructive first**: cold water → detergent → oxygen bleach →
   peroxide → solvent. Chlorine only on sturdy whites.

## 6. Dye-transfer rescue (the pink-whites accident)

1. **Do not dry.** Rewash immediately, hottest safe, with detergent and
   oxygen bleach.
2. Still pink: all-night oxygen-bleach soak (sodium percarbonate), warm.
3. Last resort on pure whites: color remover (sodium hydrosulfite) — this
   strips *all* dye including any pattern.
4. Prevention: color-catcher sheets for mixed loads; wash new dark cottons
   twice alone.

## 7. The "dry clean" myth

The plain circle means the manufacturer didn't test home laundering — it's
a liability label, not necessarily a technical requirement. Many wool and
silk items labeled dry-clean do fine with: cold water, wool/silk detergent,
5-min gentle hand wash, roll in a towel, dry flat. Test with an inside
hem first. Structured garments (canvas jackets with interfacing, lined
suits, pleated skirts) genuinely need professional pressing equipment.

## 8. Machine care notes

- Front-loaders are gentler and wash colder-better; top-loaders with
  agitators are harsher on delicates.
- Run a 60°C+ "maintenance wash" monthly (empty, detergent) to kill biofilm
  in the drum/door seal — the source of most "machine smells".
- Don't leave wet loads sitting: mildew sets within hours in a sealed drum.
